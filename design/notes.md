# Design notes

## Current direction (November 2026)
In Countless Shadows is a Godot prototype exploring Shakespeare's Sonnet 17 as playable poetry. The immediate goal is a small, compelling, portrait-first touch puzzle, not a complete curriculum or fourteen-line editor.

### Design principles
- A single clear touch interaction should support meaningful experimentation.
- Formal constraints should interact, not merely appear as separate rhyme, meter, and volta quizzes.
- A player's move should change what is possible, not simply trigger explanatory feedback.
- Formal validity is not a machine-certified correct interpretation.
- Support undo, reset, legible EB Garamond text, and minimal on-screen instruction.
- Study Zach Gage's TypeShift, Knotwords, SpellTower, and Really Bad Chess for approachable controls, legibility, and emergent depth. These are design references, not evidence of a research result.

### Candidate mechanical experiments (not committed features)
1. Two to four lines with carefully authored alternatives. Test whether changing a line or ending affects rhyme, syntax, and interpretation simultaneously.
2. Meter as an expectation that language negotiates, particularly Sonnet 17's “stretchèd meter.” Avoid simplistic ten-syllable correctness.
3. Formal connections (rhyme, stanza, volta) that change the available interpretive routes rather than only lighting up.
4. A “shadow” reading generated by a legal traversal through the poem. Test whether different traversals support meaningfully different readings.

### Advisor feedback, November 4, 2026
Stephanie urged a clear distinction between scholarship and making a successful game. The research needs an independent question, methodology, and contribution even if the game remains unfinished. Do not merely illustrate Palfrey's theory. Investigate what computation reveals, complicates, or fails to capture. Research historical practices of reading, memorizing, teaching, and interpreting poems and riddles. Ask what kind of knowledge puzzle solving produces, and whether formal success leads to interpretive judgment or only mastery of abstract rules. Treat claims about contemporary literacy, AI summaries, and changing reading practices as hypotheses requiring evidence.

### Current research-design tension
The game should offer satisfying, discoverable rules without pretending that the software can decide a poem's meaning. Even formal features depend on editorial and historical assumptions; record the assumptions encoded in each puzzle. The designer's choice of rules can reproduce interpretive authority, not automatically democratize it.

### Next prototype experiment
Build one portrait touch puzzle that allows multiple formally defensible outcomes, with a clear consequence for each move. Test it with readers before expanding the level sequence. Observe whether players notice meaning and expectation, not only rhyme or correct placement.

### Open questions
- What changes in interpretation when a reader can manipulate constraints and see immediate feedback?
- Can the game be satisfying without treating literary interpretation as a right/wrong answer?
- What does the system actually know, and which judgments are editorial?
- Can players explain different readings after arriving at the same formally valid state?
- Which observations would challenge our theory?

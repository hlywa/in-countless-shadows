# In Countless Shadows: working notes

## The problem with the current opening

Our level-select screen offers four compartments: The Turn, The Echo, The Measure, and The Whole Sonnet. These name parts of poetic technique, but give the player little reason to care about them yet. We are presenting a curriculum before a problem worth playing with. This is especially strange for Sonnet 17, whose effects depend on these features working together.

The existing scenes are useful sketches. They help us find out what Godot can draw, rearrange, and check. But four functioning exercises are not necessarily one good game.

## Start with the poem

“Who will believe my verse in time to come?” is a fear before it is a formal problem. The speaker might describe the beloved faithfully and still be accused of lying by future readers who never saw that person. The imagined papers turn yellow. The words might survive while failing to preserve what mattered.

Helen Vendler describes the poem moving into an imagined future and then retreating from it. The future first speaks in lines 7–8, when readers pronounce the poet a liar. Then, unexpectedly, the future becomes visible in line 9 as the poet sees his own yellowed pages. Vendler calls these two distinct climaxes. The second doesn't erase the first; it changes the shape of what we thought was complete.

Vendler also follows the echoes that hold this movement together: *numbers / number*, *touches / touched*, *time* in lines 1 and 13, and *life / alive / live* in lines 4, 13, and 14. The repetitions matter because of what they do to the poem's evolving argument, not because repetition itself is a collectible feature. See *The Art of Shakespeare's Sonnets*, pp. 116–118.

This is a better starting point than asking players to identify the volta. Sonnet 17 gives us an unsettling problem: **words can outlive a person without making that person believable.**

## What Knotwords clarified

The appealing part of *Knotwords* isn't the bright keyboard or the minimal screen. It's the order in which the game makes itself intelligible. A small action comes first. A constraint becomes apparent because the player needs it. More constraints arrive after the first has become familiar, and their interactions produce the difficulty.

We can teach poetic vocabulary, but it should name something the player has already experienced. “Rhyme,” “meter,” or “volta” shouldn't be prerequisites for touching the poem.

There is also an important difference: *Knotwords* can check whether words meet its rules. Our software can check some formal relationships, but it cannot authoritatively decide whether a reading is persuasive, moving, or true.

## The encounter we haven't made yet

We need a small playable problem with a consequence the player can perceive. A change to the language should do something, preferably more than one thing, rather than merely illuminate a technical term. The interesting moment may be when satisfying one constraint makes another aim harder to achieve.

The conflict between faithful description and future believability is a possible design center, not yet a scoring system. If we give “credibility” a meter, whose judgment have we encoded? If every puzzle has one approved arrangement, what sort of reading are we actually inviting?

The next prototype should stand on its own as a satisfying puzzle, without requiring an explanation of the research. We can ask players afterward what changed in their understanding, and listen for more than whether they noticed the correct pattern.

## Two different standards of success

Stephanie's November 4 feedback remains important: a worthwhile game and a worthwhile scholarly contribution are different achievements. The game cannot merely illustrate a theory we've already accepted. The research has to investigate what computation reveals, distorts, or makes impossible to see, with evidence from actual readers and with attention to historical practices of reading, recitation, and interpretation.

Vendler gives us a particularly useful warning. In her introduction, she assembles a fourteen-line patchwork of Shakespeare's language that meets the recognizable formal specifications but lacks a coherent movement of thought (pp. 4–5). She also declines to offer a full account of meter because she has not found a sufficiently subtle, communicable theory of scansion (p. 11). Our own ten-position stress guide is therefore an authored model, not a definitive test of poetic rhythm.

The question is not whether players can be trained to satisfy the rules we wrote. It is whether playing with those rules helps them notice, test, or dispute a relationship in the poem that they might otherwise have missed.
